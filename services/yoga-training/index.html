Frequently Asked Questions

Everything You Need to Know About Yoga Classes

Is yoga good for beginners?

Yes. With a teacher who offers modifications and props, yoga is one of the most beginner-friendly forms of exercise. Start with a hatha or gentle class.

How often should I practise yoga?

Two to three sessions a week suit most beginners, with short home practice on other days. Daily practice is fine if it stays gentle.

Which type of yoga is best for beginners?

Hatha and Iyengar-style classes are popular starting points because they are slower and focus on alignment. Your teacher can advise you after a short conversation.

Can I do yoga if I am not flexible?

Yes. Flexibility improves with regular practice, and props let you work at your current range.

Is yoga good for weight loss?

It can support weight loss through muscle work, stress reduction and mindful habits, but gentle yoga burns relatively little energy. It works best alongside strength training, walking and balanced eating.

Can yoga help with back pain?

Research suggests yoga may give small improvements in chronic low back pain compared with no exercise. See your doctor first for a diagnosis, and tell your teacher about any back problems.

Is yoga better in the morning or evening?

The best time is the one you will keep. Many people prefer the morning for energy and the evening for relaxation. Avoid practising straight after a heavy meal.

Are online yoga classes effective?

They can be, especially with live guidance and feedback on your form. Our online yoga classes offer 20 live sessions at ₹750.

What should I wear and bring?

Comfortable, stretchy clothing, a non-slip mat, a water bottle and a small towel.

How soon will I see results?

Many people feel calmer and looser within a few weeks. Strength, balance and posture usually change over two to three months of regular practice.

Complete Guide to Yoga Practice

Yoga for Beginners: Styles, Poses, Evidence & Building a Home Practice

Quick Answer: Yoga is a mind-body practice originating in India that unites physical postures (asanas), breathing techniques (pranayama), and relaxation or meditation. Clinical research suggests consistent yoga practice improves flexibility, balance, and muscular endurance, helps reduce perceived stress and low mood, and supports sleep quality. Most beginners start comfortably with two to three guided sessions of 30 to 60 minutes per week, using simple alignment cues and props to build mobility safely.

What Is Yoga and Why It Is Worth Your Time

The word yoga comes from the Sanskrit root yuj, meaning to join or unite. Originating in India, one of its best-known early texts—the Yoga Sutras attributed to Patanjali—describes an eight-limb path covering ethical conduct, postures, breath control, concentration, and meditation. Physical postures are only one of those eight limbs. Later hatha texts such as the Hatha Yoga Pradipika (circa 15th century) put greater focus on asana and pranayama to prepare the body and mind.

In December 2014, the United Nations General Assembly proclaimed 21 June as the International Day of Yoga following India's proposal (first observed in 2015), and in 2016 UNESCO added yoga to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) also notes that modern styles vary widely from gentle restorative work to vigorous flows, so research outcomes depend heavily on which style is studied.

Modern schedules move us far less than our bodies expect. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that around 31 percent of adults worldwide missed recommended activity targets in 2022, leaving many with tight hips, stiff shoulders, lower-back tension, and scattered focus. US national survey data cited by the NCCIH also show yoga use among adults aged 65 and older climbing from 1.3 percent in 2002 to 6.7 percent in 2017—showing how smoothly adaptable movement works across age groups.

Benefits of Yoga: What Clinical Research Suggests

Looking honestly at clinical studies clarifies where yoga excels and where marketing exaggerates:

  • Flexibility, strength, and balance: Regular yoga steadily expands joint range of motion and builds muscular endurance in the legs, core, and shoulders. Studies in community-dwelling older adults also support meaningful gains in static balance, helping reduce fall risk.
  • Perceived stress: A 2020 review of 12 studies covering 672 healthy adults—highlighted by the NCCIH—found every included study reported beneficial reductions in perceived stress following yoga practice.
  • Mood and anxiety: The NCCIH notes yoga can serve as a helpful supportive addition alongside standard care for depression and everyday anxiety symptoms, though evidence specifically for clinically diagnosed anxiety disorders remains limited. Yoga complements professional medical or psychological treatment without replacing it.
  • Sleep quality: Several clinical trials suggest improvements in sleep onset and subjective sleep quality, particularly with evening routines built around gentle stretching, restorative postures, and slow breathing.
  • Chronic low back pain: A landmark 2022 Cochrane systematic review examining 21 trials across 2,223 adults with chronic non-specific low back pain found yoga performed slightly better than doing no exercise at three months, though the average pain and function improvement was modest. Head-to-head against other structured back-focused exercise, yoga produced broadly similar outcomes, with temporary back pain flare-ups reported as the most frequent adverse event.
  • Migraine and knee osteoarthritis: A review of six trials involving 445 participants linked regular yoga with lower migraine intensity and frequency. Meanwhile, the 2022 COPE randomised trial of 212 adults with knee osteoarthritis found an unsupervised 12-week online programme improved physical function and stiffness, though walking pain did not drop significantly compared with education alone—reminding us that guided progression matters.

Types of Yoga: Choosing the Right Style and Pace

Because yoga spans everything from motionless supported rest to fast continuous flows, picking the right class matters. Below is how common styles compare, including verified energy-expenditure estimates from the Compendium of Physical Activities (where 1 MET equals sitting quietly at rest):

Essential Yoga Poses (Asanas) & Beginner Progression

Nine core postures form the backbone of safe beginner practice, each adjustable to your joints:

  • Mountain pose (Tadasana): Standing tall with balanced weight across both feet; teaches neutral spinal posture and body awareness.
  • Cat-cow (Marjaryasana-Bitilasana): Rhythmic spinal flexion and extension on hands and knees (with a folded towel under knees if needed) to ease morning back stiffness.
  • Downward-facing dog (Adho Mukha Svanasana): Lengthens calves, hamstrings, and upper back; bending knees generously protects tight lower backs.
  • Warrior II (Virabhadrasana II): Builds leg endurance, hip opening, and upper-back posture.
  • Tree pose (Vrikshasana): Trains single-leg stability and focus; rest your lifted foot firmly against the ankle or calf—never directly onto the knee joint.
  • Cobra (Bhujangasana): A gentle prone backbend strengthening spinal extensors while opening chest muscles tightened by laptops.
  • Bridge pose (Setu Bandhasana): Activates glutes and hamstrings while lightly opening hip flexors.
  • Child’s pose (Balasana) & Corpse pose (Shavasana): Restorative pauses you can visit mid-class and at closing to integrate breath and calm.

Surya Namaskar (Sun Salutation) connects twelve rhythmic postures in sequence. Done deliberately with breath cues, five slow rounds warm every major joint in ten minutes. When starting out, remember six simple guidelines: begin with two or three sessions of 20 to 40 minutes weekly; prioritise joint alignment over touching the floor; use props like cushions, belts, or a wall without hesitation; breathe steadily rather than holding your breath; ease out immediately if you feel sharp, pinching, or burning joint pain; and stay consistent—fifteen calm minutes four days a week outperforms one heroic weekend marathon.

Pranayama: Practical Breathing Exercises

Pranayama refers to conscious regulation of breath. Slow, deliberate breathing around roughly six breaths per minute has been widely studied for enhancing heart-rate variability and encouraging parasympathetic relaxation. Beginners start safely with three gentle staples:

  • Diaphragmatic (belly) breathing: Letting the lower ribs and belly expand gently on inhales so neck and chest muscles stop overworking.
  • Nadi shodhana (alternate-nostril breathing): Alternating slow inhales and exhales quietly between nostrils to settle scattered attention.
  • Bhramari (humming bee breath): A steady, soft humming exhale that lengthens exhalation naturally and works wonderfully before bed.

Forceful rapid breathing or prolonged breath retention (kumbhaka) are not appropriate for everyone. Clients with heart conditions, lung disorders, high blood pressure, or pregnancy should consult their doctor first and learn gentle pacing progressively. Yoga for Weight Loss: Honest Expectations

Many people sign up for yoga hoping it will melt belly fat rapidly on its own. Knowing the numbers saves disappointment. In the Compendium of Physical Activities, gentle hatha yoga averages around 2.5 METs—comparable to an easy stroll—while faster Power Yoga reaches roughly 4.0 METs and Surya Namaskar sits around 3.3–3.5 METs. One relaxed weekly class will not create a large calorie deficit by itself.

Yet research still shows yoga supports weight management through complementary habits. A review of 17 yoga-based weight-control interventions noted modest, gradual weight reductions, with the strongest results appearing in programmes featuring longer and more frequent sessions, regular home practice, a mindful dietary component, and broader lifestyle elements beyond postures alone. Safety Precautions, Myths vs Facts & Your Balanced Week

Safety & Medical Guidance: The NCCIH notes yoga carries a low side-effect rate overall, though rare nerve or vascular injuries occur alongside mild soreness or temporary back strain—usually from forcing depth. Speak with your doctor first if you have high blood pressure, glaucoma, sciatica, osteoporosis, recent surgery, or heart/lung conditions. If pregnant, tell your instructor immediately so deep twists, supine compresses, and overheating are adapted. Stop immediately for chest pain, dizziness, fainting, or numbness.

  • Myth: “I am not flexible enough for yoga.” Flexibility develops through regular practice; props adapt every pose to your current mobility.
  • Myth: “Yoga is only stretching.” Holding warrior stances, planks, bridges, and slow transitions builds legitimate muscular endurance and balance.
  • Myth: “Yoga is only for women.” Historically developed and practised heavily by men, yoga strengthens joints, posture, and stress resilience for everyone.
  • Myth: “Yoga requires adopting religious beliefs.” While rooted in Indian philosophy, modern fitness and health classes are practical, inclusive, and secular.
  • Myth: “If it does not hurt, it does not work.” Mild muscle effort is productive; sharp joint pain is a clear warning to back off immediately.

Finally, remember how yoga fits your broader weekly health target: the WHO advises adults to complete 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity plus two or more days of muscle-strengthening work weekly (with three or more days of balance and strength emphasis for adults 65+).
