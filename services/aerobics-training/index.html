Frequently Asked Questions

Everything You Need to Know About Aerobics Classes

Is aerobics good for weight loss?

Yes, especially when paired with balanced nutrition and strength training. Exercise alone yields modest weight changes unless weekly volume is high, so combining cardio with practical eating works best.

How often should I do aerobics?

Most adults do well with three to five sessions per week, gradually building toward the World Health Organization guideline of 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity.

Is aerobics suitable for beginners?

Yes. Low-impact aerobics options keep one foot grounded at all times, making rhythmic movement easy to follow at your own pace without high jumping stress.

What is the difference between aerobic and anaerobic exercise?

Aerobic exercise means working with oxygen in steady, sustained rhythms like brisk walking, dancing, or step classes. Anaerobic exercise uses brief, intense bursts such as sprinting or heavy lifting.

Can I do aerobics if I have knee pain?

Often yes, using low-impact steps and cushioned cross-training footwear, provided you consult your doctor or physiotherapist first and notify your instructor.

Are online aerobics classes effective?

Yes, particularly with live instruction that watches your posture and pacing. Our online aerobics classes include 20 guided sessions at ₹750.

What is the difference between low-impact and high-impact aerobics?

Low-impact aerobics keeps at least one foot on the floor continuously to reduce joint shock, whereas high-impact aerobics adds hops, jacks, and jumps for higher intensity.

How much space and equipment do I need at home?

About two metres by two metres of clear floor space, supportive cushioned shoes, and a bottle of water are enough for an effective home aerobics workout.

What should I wear and bring for a session?

Wear breathable athletic clothing and cushioned cross-training shoes rather than flat canvas sneakers, and keep water, a towel, and a mat for stretching nearby.

How soon will I feel fitter?

Many beginners notice steadier energy and less breathlessness on stairs within two to four weeks of regular sessions.

Complete Guide to Aerobic Exercise

Aerobics for Beginners & Weight Loss: Science, Intensity Zones & Safe Progression

Quick Answer: Aerobic exercise is rhythmic, sustained movement—such as aerobics classes, brisk walking, cycling, or dance cardio—that elevates heart rate and breathing using oxygen to generate energy. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week (or 75 to 150 minutes vigorous) for adults. Regular aerobics strengthens cardiovascular endurance, aids weight management alongside nutrition, improves sleep and mood, and scales safely for beginners through low-impact variations.

What Is Aerobics and How Does a Class Work?

“Aerobic” literally means “with oxygen.” During aerobic exercise, your muscles draw continuously on oxygen delivered by your lungs and heart to burn carbohydrates and fats over prolonged periods. That contrasts with anaerobic efforts—like a max-effort sprint or heavy barbell set—which burn fuel quickly over seconds without relying primarily on oxygen uptake. Physician Dr. Kenneth H. Cooper popularised the concept globally through his landmark 1968 book Aerobics, shifting preventive fitness toward measurable cardiovascular endurance.

A guided aerobics class strings together simple, repeatable patterns—marching in place, side step-touches, grapevines, alternating knee lifts, gentle kicks, and coordinated arm swings—set to upbeat music typically between 120 and 150 beats per minute. Because combinations repeat in straightforward blocks, first-timers pick up steps within minutes while experienced participants work brisker ranges or higher knee drives in the very same session.

Benefits of Aerobic Exercise Backed by Research

Cardiovascular conditioning sits among the most thoroughly studied areas in preventive health. The World Health Organization notes regular physical activity helps prevent and manage heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and several cancers, while supporting healthy body weight and mental wellbeing. Yet WHO estimates for 2022 showed roughly 31 percent of adults worldwide fell short of recommended activity targets—usually due to hectic routines or lack of structure rather than lack of desire.

  • Heart and lung capacity: Over weeks, your heart muscle pumps slightly more blood per beat, daily errands feel less taxing, and resting heart rate often settles lower.
  • Stamina for real life: Climbing two flights of stairs, carrying groceries, commuting, and keeping pace with children feel noticeably smoother.
  • Weight control support: Sustained movement lifts weekly calorie burn and improves insulin sensitivity when paired with sensible eating.
  • Mood and sleep: Regular aerobic activity consistently links with lower perceived stress, fewer anxiety and depressive symptoms, and steadier sleep depth.
  • Bone and joint resilience: Weight-bearing rhythm strengthens leg bones, while controlled low-impact movement lubricates joints gently without pounding.

How Much Aerobic Exercise Do You Need? (Intensity & METs Table)

For adults aged 18–64, the WHO recommends at least 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise weekly, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity work (or an equivalent blend), alongside muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days and less uninterrupted sitting. For adults aged 65 and over, the WHO additionally advises multi-component balance and functional strength work on three or more days per week.

Aerobics for Weight Loss: What to Expect Honestly

Aerobics is an excellent calorie-burning tool—with one critical caveat about expectations. According to the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), 150 to 250 minutes per week of moderate physical activity prevents weight gain and delivers only modest weight loss on its own, whereas exceeding 250 minutes weekly aligns with clinically significant loss when diet is uncontrolled. That is why relying solely on marathon cardio sessions without reviewing meals often leads to frustration.

Pairing three to four online aerobics sessions a week with practical Indian food portions and two brief strength workouts produces far better body composition. Resistance work helps retain lean muscle while fat declines, and balanced meals prevent post-cardio rebound snacking. Interval blocks—brief twenty-to-thirty-second brisk bursts followed by active marching recovery—boost cardiovascular fitness efficiently once a foundation is built, though steady low-impact sessions work wonderfully for beginners first. Types of Aerobics Classes & Choosing Yours

  • Low-impact aerobics: Keeps one foot grounded at all times—ideal for complete beginners, heavier frames, older adults, knee sensitivity, and postpartum return after doctor approval.
  • High-impact aerobics: Introduces controlled hops, jacks, and light bounding for stronger cardiovascular and bone stimulus once joints are prepared.
  • Step aerobics: Uses a low adjustable platform to increase leg engagement and calorie demand; platform height starts at 4 inches and stays optional.
  • Dance aerobics: Blends rhythmic choreography with music for social, joyful energy where forty-five minutes fly by.
  • Circuit and interval aerobics: Alternates rhythmic cardio sequences with bodyweight strength moves like squats, wall push-ups, and glute bridges.
  • Chair / senior-friendly aerobics: Seated or wall-supported upper- and lower-body rhythmic patterns tailored for balance confidence and limited mobility.

Inside a Session & 4-Week Beginner Progression

A standard 45-to-60-minute class flows across four clear phases: 5–10 minutes of gentle marching, shoulder rolls, and ankle mobility to warm muscles; 20–40 minutes of progressive rhythmic cardio blocks with easier and brisker options side by side; 5–10 minutes of slowing steps to bring heart rate down smoothly; and targeted stretches for calves, hamstrings, hips, and chest.

Throughout every session, Sandhya tracks breathing, alignment, and fatigue so nobody feels pressured to match the fastest pace in the room. Remember three posture habits from day one: keep your chest lifted so lungs expand freely, hold a soft bend in your knees rather than locking joints straight, and roll quietly through the ball of your foot onto your heel instead of stomping flat-footed.

If you are restarting after months at a desk, follow a steady four-week ramp rather than pushing hard on day one. A sensible guideline is raising total weekly cardio minutes by no more than roughly 10 percent at a time so calves, Achilles tendons, and knee cartilage adapt smoothly:

  • Week 1 (Learn the rhythm): Three 15-to-20-minute low-impact blocks focusing on marching, side steps, gentle knee lifts, and keeping breathing comfortable on the talk test.
  • Week 2 (Build continuity): Three to four 25-minute sessions adding controlled arm reaches and grapevines while staying entirely low-impact.
  • Week 3 (Lengthen endurance): Four 30-to-35-minute sessions weaving light brisker tempo intervals or optional low step touches into the middle blocks.
  • Week 4 (Full class comfort): Four to five 40-to-45-minute classes moving toward the WHO 150-minute weekly baseline, resting comfortably between harder days.

Mild warmth and tiredness in large thigh or calf muscles for a day after class is completely normal. Sharp, pinching, or swelling sensations around the kneecap, shin, or ankle are clear signals to pause, switch to grounded marching, and inspect footwear or technique.

Online Aerobics at Home: Space, Footwear, Floors & Hydration

Home aerobics requires remarkably little: roughly two metres by two metres of clear floor space, breathable clothing, and a stable screen for live form cues. At ₹750 for 20 online sessions, attending three to five classes a week becomes realistic without losing an hour in traffic.

Pay special attention to shoes and surfaces—especially in Indian homes with marble, vitrified tile, or concrete flooring. Avoid barefoot jumping on hard tile. Wear cushioned cross-training or fitness sneakers (not thin flat canvas shoes) and place a sturdy, non-slip mat underneath if your floor is slick. During hot and humid Indian summers or monsoons, hydrate before, during, and after class, avoid working out in unventilated noon heat, and take five minutes to cool down gently before stepping directly into cold air-conditioning.

Safety Guidelines, Myths vs Facts & Your Weekly Balance

Medical Clearance & Warning Signs: Most healthy adults can start gentle-to-moderate aerobic exercise safely, though you should obtain medical approval first if you have heart disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure, diabetes, asthma or lung disease, joint injuries, recent surgery, or are pregnant. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) encourages around 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly during uncomplicated pregnancies with medical clearance. Stop exercising immediately and seek urgent care if you notice chest pain or tightness, severe shortness of breath, dizziness, fainting, irregular heartbeats, or pain radiating into your arm or jaw.

  • Myth: “Aerobics is only for women.” Cardiovascular health, blood pressure, and stamina benefit men and women equally.
  • Myth: “You must do aerobics daily.” Three to five weekly classes leave room for recovery and strength training.
  • Myth: “More sweat equals faster fat loss.” Sweat reflects heat dissipation and humidity, not calories burned; replenish fluids rather than chasing dehydration.
  • Myth: “Aerobics ruins knees.” Low-impact stepping, supportive shoes, and controlled landings typically support joint nourishment rather than wearing them down.
  • Myth: “Cardio alone shapes the whole body.” Strength training twice a week plus yoga mobility and meditation recovery round out a resilient body.
